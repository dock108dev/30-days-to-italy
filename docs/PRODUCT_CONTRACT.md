# Product Contract — 30 Days to Italy

This contract is governed by the two-mode lifecycle in [`PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md`](PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md). Existing encounter rules remain valid, but the 30 sessions now prepare for a 7–10 day trip rather than depicting a literal 30-day vacation.

## Product promise

**Help a reserved traveler rehearse likely moments before Italy, then carry the most useful practiced language into the trip as a personal Pocket Deck.**

The visible objective is always a life objective. The game may be trying to reactivate a request structure or test listening under noise, but the player is trying to obtain a key, catch a ferry, fix an order, or leave an interaction politely.

## Target learner

The first player is an English-speaking adult who:

- completed introductory Italian years ago and retains more recognition than active recall;
- has limited recent experience in Italy;
- has extensive Spanish study that can intrude into word choice and sentence shape;
- reads better than they understand natural speech;
- is not naturally conversational and does not want performance of sociability treated as progress;
- wants enough listening comprehension and practical control for a 7–10 day solo vacation, with a possible longer stay later; and
- will initially listen and type, without speaking or pronunciation scoring.

The design can later serve adjacent learners, but it must not dilute this concrete learner into a generic proficiency persona.

## Player fantasy

The player’s trip begins emotionally before the flight. Each day of preparation makes a likely vacation moment more familiar: hotel arrival, beach rental, ordering, payment, transportation, problems, and optional familiarity. The simulation is a rehearsal space, not a claim that its fictional dates and prices are live travel facts.

On departure, the useful parts of that practice become a personal Pocket Deck. The fantasy is not “I mastered Italian.” It is: **I prepared for the moments that matter, I can recover when the script breaks, and I have immediate help built from my own practice.**

## Product lifecycle contract

- **Rehearsal Mode** teaches through guided, consequence-bearing simulations before departure.
- **Trip Mode** provides fast, offline-friendly cards during the actual trip.
- Rehearsal evidence selects and improves Pocket Deck cards.
- Trip Mode does not show lessons, corrections, scores, or simulated consequences.
- The departure date drives prioritization; missed sessions never create streak debt.
- The 30 preparation sessions do not represent 30 literal vacation days.
- The first lifecycle proof is incomplete until one rehearsal creates a card that is useful in Trip Mode.

## Non-goals

This product is not:

- a comprehensive Italian curriculum or CEFR certification path;
- a vocabulary quiz, grammar syllabus, phrasebook, or translation drill collection;
- a social or dating simulator;
- a tourism database dependent on current real businesses or timetables;
- a sandbox in which a language model invents money, commitments, or consequences;
- a points, hearts, streak, leaderboard, or daily-pressure system;
- a pronunciation tutor in the first version;
- an open-ended chatbot with Italian scenery; or
- a platform for many languages, regions, learner types, or community content.

## Daily gameplay loop

Each approximately ten-minute episode follows a stable but not rigid loop:

1. **Orient.** See the practical objective, current place and time, and any known constraints.
2. **Listen.** Hear one short NPC turn at realistic speed, normally without an exposed transcript.
3. **Choose support.** Replay, request careful-speed playback, or reveal the Italian transcript. Support is available without shame but its use is remembered for adaptation.
4. **Respond.** Type any plausible attempt: complete Italian, a short fragment, mixed Italian and English nouns, a clarification request, or an exit.
5. **Interpret safely.** The conversation system proposes communicative intent, key details, confidence, and possible repair—not world-state changes.
6. **Resolve.** Authored rules validate a bounded outcome and apply permitted changes to time, money, reservations, inventory, commitments, facts, and relationships.
7. **Reflect briefly.** If useful, show what was understood, one natural expression, and one contextually relevant variation.
8. **Leave or continue.** End as soon as the objective permits, or take a clearly optional conversational opening.
9. **Carry forward.** Save outcomes, support use, unresolved commitments, character memories, evidence about communicative competence, and the Pocket Deck card added or improved by the session.

One NPC line and one player response form the default action cycle. Multi-turn repair is used only when the practical objective genuinely requires it.

When the player uses a fully English action frame or explicitly asks for help, the product temporarily steps out of the NPC conversation. It teaches one reusable Italian building block, preserves the current turn without mutation, and returns the player to the same practical moment. This is guided support, not an NPC becoming a tutor.

## Player agency contract

The player may always, when contextually plausible:

- use the shortest authentic response that accomplishes the task;
- decline an offer or additional conversation;
- ask for repetition, clarification, time, or a later discussion;
- interrupt politely when the NPC has misunderstood or is over-explaining;
- embed an English noun in a useful Italian frame;
- accept an imperfect or costly outcome rather than optimize the scene;
- leave after the transaction is complete; and
- choose warmth or curiosity without making it mandatory.

The interface must distinguish **required repair** (“the clerk does not know which booking”) from **optional continuation** (“the bartender asks about the player’s day”). Silence, brevity, and leaving are designed choices, not lesser play styles.

## NPC interaction rules

NPCs must:

- pursue a believable role-based goal rather than act as tutors;
- keep each turn short enough to process by ear;
- accept concise language when intent and essential details are clear;
- ask only the minimum follow-up needed to resolve ambiguity;
- respond to what the player meant before offering correction;
- honor exits and conversational boundaries immediately;
- remember only authored facts and validated prior events;
- surface optional familiarity without demanding reciprocity; and
- remain within encounter-specific knowledge, tone, patience, and allowed outcomes.

NPCs must not:

- quiz for facts they do not need;
- withhold a valid outcome to elicit a target phrase;
- manufacture small talk after the objective is complete;
- treat Spanish leakage, spelling, or missing agreement as total failure;
- silently change a price, booking, relationship, or promise; or
- invent an unavailable item, service, route, place, or callback.

### Design lesson: the apartment-manager failure

An exhausted player asks for the key. The manager understands enough to identify the player and the apartment, but continues asking where the player came from, why they are in Italy, how long they will stay, or what grammatical form they intended. This is a failed encounter even if it creates more Italian output.

The practical contract is: once identity and entitlement are adequately resolved, the manager provides the key, essential access information, and a natural exit. One necessary clarification is acceptable. Pedagogical questioning is not. Optional chat can be offered only after the key is in the player’s possession and must be dismissible with `Sono stanco. Ne parliamo domani?`, `Più tardi, grazie`, or a simple thank-you and departure.

## Audio and transcript behavior

- The first presentation of a turn is audio at realistic conversational speed.
- Replay and careful-speed playback are available throughout all 30 days. Their controls may become less prominent as evidence grows, but they are not removed.
- The Italian transcript is hidden initially and revealed only by player action. English translation is not a default encounter support; it may appear after resolution or after repeated failed repair.
- Revealing a transcript does not reduce an arbitrary score or block success. It becomes diagnostic evidence for future support.
- Transcript timing, punctuation, and speaker labeling must accurately match the audio.
- Ambient sound can add realism only when the target line remains intelligible and the listening challenge is authored.
- Repeated encounters revisit comparable acoustic demands so reduced support has meaning.

The planned fade is described in the learning model; the individual encounter always declares the supports it permits.

## Correction behavior

Correction follows a maximum three-part pattern:

1. **What we understood** — confirm or clarify the communicative intent.
2. **A natural way to say it** — one short expression suited to the moment.
3. **One useful variation** — for example, more polite, casual, firm, warm, or precise.

Only the highest-value issue is addressed. A successful transaction is never paused for a grammar lecture. A meaning-changing correction appears automatically after practical resolution. Naturalness-only suggestions remain collapsed unless requested, and no correction is needed when the player deliberately uses an already-good fragment.

Spanish-shaped Italian is treated as evidence: preserve the valid communicative core, label the likely crossover briefly, and give the Italian contrast. English nouns are accepted when the surrounding Italian carries the move; the Italian noun can be modeled without invalidating the result.

## Success, partial success, and failure

There is no universal numeric score. An episode records a practical outcome and evidence.

- **Successful:** the objective is achieved under acceptable consequences.
- **Partial:** the objective is achieved with a cost, delay, awkwardness, unnecessary support, or unresolved detail.
- **Clever:** the player finds an authored alternative that was not the most direct path.
- **Misunderstood:** the NPC acts on a plausible but incorrect interpretation, with a chance to notice and repair when appropriate.
- **Failed:** the practical objective is not achieved, but the world remains coherent and a later recovery path exists.
- **Abandoned:** the player intentionally exits; this may be a valid boundary even when the objective remains unresolved.

Support use changes the evidence, not the legitimacy of the life outcome. Failure must be informative and recoverable, never a dead end designed to punish imperfect language.

## Persistent rehearsal-world principles

- The game, not the language model, owns canonical reality.
- Every mutation derives from a validated authored outcome.
- Money, time, location, possessions, reservations, obligations, availability, known facts, and relationship state persist.
- Characters remember selected, authored facts and the player’s validated conduct; they do not retain raw conversation as unrestricted memory.
- Promises have a responsible party, due window, status, and callback possibilities.
- Relationships are qualitative and legible—recognition, trust, irritation, debt, warmth—not a hidden affection meter.
- An episode may branch or become unavailable based on state, but must provide an authored fallback when required for season continuity.
- Replay for practice does not silently rewrite the canonical timeline. The player explicitly chooses canonical restart, non-canonical practice, or admin simulation where allowed.
- Rehearsal-world details remain fictional and cannot be presented as current facts about the real trip.
- Only selected, stable language help and personal reminders move into the Pocket Deck.

## Representative encounter examples

### Minimum-language success

The hotel clerk asks: `Quante notti rimane?` The player types `Due notti.` The clerk confirms, gives the key, and states breakfast time. The response is complete for the clerk’s need. No expansion is demanded.

### Mixed-language success with a useful model

At a rental kiosk, the player types `Vorrei un umbrella per oggi.` The system infers a request for one umbrella for the day. The rental is available and the outcome proceeds. Afterward: “We understood: one umbrella for today. Natural: `Vorrei un ombrellone per oggi.` More direct: `Un ombrellone per oggi, grazie.`”

### Ambiguity that requires repair

The cashier asks whether the player wants to pay now for both items. The player types `Sì, questo.` It is unclear whether “this” means one item or the card. The cashier points to the two items and asks one bounded clarification. The game does not guess a charge.

### Optional familiarity

A familiar bartender remembers that the ferry was cancelled earlier and asks `Alla fine, com'è andata?` The required objective is only to pay. The player may say `Bene, grazie. Con la carta`, give a one-sentence account, ask a follow-up, or say `Te lo racconto un'altra volta.` Every path is legitimate; only validated facts become character memory.
